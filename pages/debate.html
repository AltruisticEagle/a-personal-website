Coaching, judging, organizing

Coaching is quite fun. Honestly, it's a skill in and of itself to abstract something you know and teach the fun parts to the next generation that would want to learn it. A tip I would give to anyone trying to teach is to focus on practice, not in the way of dropping novices straight into a speech, but in the way of using fun activities and drills.

I have a surprising judging resume in the sense it's both formal and informal. On the one hand, I've not judged much at any tournament except for the ones where I am needed as a senior member of the tournament committee. On the other hand, they literally made me a trainee panelist at our provincial debate championship because our school, as the host, needed to send a judge as a sign of good faith.

Organizing debate tournaments are fun. You get to be the judge of other people's fates. It's honestly refreshing, escaping the habitual rat race as a debater and getting to be that metaphorical judge deciding when to start and end a round.

What debate gave me:

Three life lessions I learned from debate

First: stuff about succeeding

Watching your friends succeed can be the most torturous thing ever. Is it so good to see your friends succeed, clinching tournament wins and elimination round appearances, when you're in a class with them and trying to improve together and when you're not doing the same? It's a horrible feeling that I was constantly immersed in. Indeed, that's why I quit.

How to deal with it, you may ask? I'll talk about it in the next two sections. However, I find it useful for you to know to take my advice with a grain of salt, seeing as I decided to throw this mortal coil off of my shoulders.

When you no longer have that mentality, you are cooked at the top. A good mentality is so important to success - I believe that I not only lost my ability to keep up because I wasn't willing to dedicate as much time as I can to the activity, but also when I became convinced that it's impossible to catch back up. Believe in yourself. I never did and I paid the price; in fact, I'm still paying it even as I'm dragging myself kicking and screaming away from its negative consequences.

Second: effort

Practice practice practice. Debate is an activity of constant exposure and practice. You always need to be practicing, be it practice debates or drills before your notes all by yourself.

I had a friend who went from good to great via a surprising means. They did a hundred practice Prime Minister speeches. Afterwards, they became a champion. I mean it. I saw the change happen right in front of my eyes.

So practice. The circuit is a grind. So keep up with it.

Third: how to walk away from something (why I chose to move on)

What did I do to completely liberate myself from this activity I no longer loved? It was quite a big process.

The first thing I did was to give everything I do outside of school a good, hard look. And then I realized something. Debate doesn't align with what I want to do for University. So that was the fact that got the ball rolling for me. Therein lies a lesson: whenever you feel like something isn't working for you, have a thought about whether you need it a lot, or maybe not that much! Knowing that, it's easier to pursue things that align with both the column of "I want to do this" and "this is also good for me to pursue".

The second thing I did was to just wrap up some loose ends, not abruptly but slowly. First, I decided that a natural end to my debate career would be the beginning of summer vacation back in June of this year (2026), as that's when all my club commitments and coaching sessions also end, offering me a good opportunity to just... not renew, and transition out. Second, I decided to make the most of the final few classes and tournament runs - have fun during them and be less serious during them, if you will. I'll say this right here also, there is no point in quitting cold turkey. It won't work and it won't offer you much closure. End it naturally, not abruptly.

The third and last thing I did is to figure out what I should do after I leave. For me, that was coding, exemplified by my idea to spend thirty whole hours of my life working on this challenge. Always have a way out. I didn't quit debate just to not do anything. I shifted priorities, if you will.

The liberating feeling it offered me was immense. I finally can do something I really, really like.

If you don't like doing something, consider stop doing it.
